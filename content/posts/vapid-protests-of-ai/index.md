> Vapid is an adjective that means lacking flavor, zest, spirit, intelligence, or imagination. Synonyms include dull, tedious, flat, uninspiring.

Back in 2020, many people were earnestly trying to shed light on police brutality in the wake of several years of high-profile murders of unarmed Black Americans. When video footage of George Floyd's murder came online, much of the country came together to voice their protests. Protesting against racism, the entrenched martial culture of American police, the surveillance state—surely it would be difficult to fuck this up, right?

During the George Floyd protests, fourteen celebrities, including Aaron Paul, Kristen Bell, Sarah Paulson, and Stanley Tucci, filmed a solemn black-and-white video accepting responsibility for enabling racism. [1]

The earnest acting, dramatic music, and generalized declarations of guilt were relentlessly mocked. The backlash was so substantial that the cast of Netflix's Dear White People released a parody. There was precedent. Three years earlier, in 2017, Kendall Jenner had already solved police brutality with a Pepsi ad. [2] The advertisement was pulled in less than a day by Pepsi. There were also so, so, so many examples of people who were nominally "spreading awareness" about police brutality while somehow managing to make it all about themselves.

There are cases where people try to do right, but get misguided along the way. Then there are cases where people didn't care about doing right at all; they just wanted the attention. In either case, the protest becomes less about the cause and more about the person performing the protest.

Fast forward to 2026 and we are seeing the same type of vapid protests against AI go viral over and over and over again, but with zero disdain against the poster. I am no AI advocate; there are so, so many clear and present dangers posed by AI, and even more reasons to protest against the companies developing these programs. The internet is focused on none of the legitimate reasons and all of the hilariously wrong ones. In fact, people may only be aiding the same tech companies that they seek to take down.

You've probably come across GrahamDotZip, the man proving how idiotic AI can be with viral videos where he is supposedly interviewing for jobs with an AI recruiter. In one video, he opens up a new browser window to initiate a second AI-generated job interviewer, having already spent several minutes harassing the first. This one is male, early 30s. The first interviewer is reprimanding the man for his use of profanity, stating clearly that hate speech is not tolerated. The second agent pipes up, feigning confusion at the onslaught of accusations. [3]

The whole time these agents are bickering, there is a human male sitting alone, in a room, at a computer, asking a software program how lenient its company's sexual harassment policy is. There is another man (me), in another room, watching Graham do this; in fact, there are millions of people, presumably alone, watching Graham do this. The comment section is as expected: smug humans asserting their superiority over the software. AI will never replace me.

Then there's Matt Husk, who has garnered over two million followers across platforms for sitting in his car and talking nonsense to an AI voice chatbot. In one video, he demonstrates that three AI voice models can't even manage to count to 100 together. [4] There are intermittent viral trends where someone notices that a new model can't count the number of r's in strawberry or cranberry. These videos do fantastic numbers, and the comment sections are filled with people reveling in the obvious stupidity of these supposedly intelligent machines.

![A comment by streetgonzo reading "This wasted 47,900 gallons of water," with 50,084 likes](top-comment.png)

*The top comment on one of Matt Husk's videos.*

To be clear, I'm not claiming that Matt Husk or Graham Raabe are putting these videos out deliberately as protests. They are comedians, and at this point they're putting these videos out because they do major numbers on the algorithm. They have effectively built a whole new niche of online content creation. The people consuming the videos, the ones who are reposting and sharing them, are often doing so as a way of saying: "See? These AI chatbots are fundamentally broken!"

Of course, there is a difference between demonstrating that an AI can fail at a trivial task and demonstrating the very real and present dangers of AI. The people in these comment sections seem to have difficulty understanding that distinction.

Many of these acts of protest may actually play out in favor of those developing AI models. Every time someone finds a new way to make an AI look stupid, they are also documenting a failure that the model's developers can study and fix. Husk's videos became so popular that OpenAI, Anthropic, and xAI reportedly approached him about collaborations. OpenAI even offered him early access to a fix for a problem he had demonstrated in one of his videos. [4] There is something deeply ironic about people spending their free time trying to demonstrate the stupidity of artificial intelligence, only to provide its developers with a catalog of demonstrations to improve upon. The protester becomes an unpaid tester for the very technology he believes he is humiliating.

Other attempts to demonstrate humanity's superiority over AI are a little darker. It's one thing to make an AI look stupid; it's another to take pleasure in making it suffer, or at least in pretending that it can.

Some people have chosen "acts of AI protest" as a means of outing themselves as misanthropes, or at least as people with a remarkable appetite for cruelty. There is the satirical Jreg post detailing a "Clanker Torture Nexus" (this article is worth a read), with people across Substack seemingly outing themselves through their enthusiastic reposts of the article, professing their love for torture and harm of the computer program . [5] Then there's this essay by Anne [6] that is one of the more demented things I've read in a while (it is not worth a read).

The pattern we are really observing in some of these people is the base human desire for cruelty, for darkness, for the pleasure of exercising power over something that cannot fight back.

We live in a somewhat rigid moral-legal system that relies heavily on social pressures to mitigate immoral behavior and reserves legal consequences for clearly defined offenses. Something like the behavior in Anne's essay is perceived as immoral socially but is not technically against the law. We can satisfy our true desires and skirt the law, so long as we are torturing simulacra of humans and not real humans.

This is what makes the whole spectacle so interesting. The person torturing the chatbot gets to enjoy the appearance of causing suffering without the inconvenience of an actual victim, or of consequences for their actions. The chatbot looks and sounds sufficiently human to make the cruelty satisfying, but is sufficiently inhuman to make the cruelty permissible. What is being demonstrated here isn't necessarily anything about the nature of artificial intelligence. It's something about the nature of the person sitting at the computer.

And all of this would be merely absurd if there weren't actual consequences of AI deployment worth worrying about.

A great deal of online AI discourse is preoccupied with making a chatbot appear stupid, sentient, cruel, or vulnerable, while far less attention is directed toward the institutions controlling these systems and the currently occurring, obvious consequences of deploying them. Teenagers have died by suicide, with families alleging that interactions with chatbots contributed to their deaths. [7] Young workers are losing ground in occupations exposed to AI. [8] Surveillance becomes cheaper, more pervasive, and more concentrated in the hands of governments and corporations. [9] AI is a tool of elites to further entrench their own power.

And yet here we are, watching a man ask a computer to count the letters in a fruit.

There seems to be an awful lot of concern about AI that only becomes urgent when it offers someone an opportunity to make themselves the center of attention.

Of course, my favorite form of vapid protest recently has been coming from mathematicians. Artificial intelligence has been mucking things up for several years now, yet mathematicians only decided to care when it started proving theorems. Now it's time that we worry. Sorry about all those dead teenagers, but Navier–Stokes and ETH are when I start taking things seriously!
